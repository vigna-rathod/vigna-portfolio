UX Case Study  ·  Curam Care  ·  2022–2023

Designing trust into a two‑sided care marketplace, one manual process at a time.

A ten-month research-to-redesign engagement that found why carers were leaving a UK care platform, then rebuilt onboarding, job discovery, messaging, and the design system to fix it for both sides of the marketplace.

01

Context & the problem

Curam lets care receivers post jobs and independent carers choose which ones to take — better pay and more autonomy than a traditional care agency. I led design for ten months, working directly with UK stakeholders across the carer app, the care-receiver app, and the design system underneath both.

  • Stakeholders' working theory: carers were dropping off because they weren't "tech-savvy enough" for a digital platform.
  • My job was to test that theory before designing against it — a research-led engagement, not a straight redesign.
  • It turned out to be almost entirely wrong.
A note on the two sides of this marketplace

Formal research in this case study is carer-side — carers were the group actively leaving the platform, so that's where discovery started. But Curam is a two-sided marketplace, and carer interviews kept surfacing problems that only a care-receiver-facing redesign could fix — vague job posts, and receivers too afraid of a stranger to message carers at all. So this case study covers discovery on the carer side, my design reasoning on the care-receiver side, and the shared systems work underneath both. Every section is tagged so it's clear whose experience is shown.

A platform built to feel like independence for the carer — running on a stack of manual, back-office decisions neither side could see.
02

Discovery — the carer side

Carer-facing research

Research covered the entire carer lifecycle — not just onboarding — because early conversations kept surfacing problems downstream of signup.

  • 19 problem areas mapped: landing page, onboarding, verification, categorization, job search, messaging, intro meetings, service agreements, invoicing, payments, and more.
  • Each area produced its own "How Might We" statements — the mechanism that let the team prioritize with stakeholders instead of guessing.
Key finding

Carers weren't struggling with the interface. They were struggling with an absence of information the system already had, but never surfaced — why a field was needed, whether a job was still open, why they'd been marked unavailable, when they'd get paid.

03

Discovery — the care-receiver side

Care-receiver-facing reasoning

No formal interview study existed for care receivers — this side came from what carer interviews revealed about the other end of every conversation, plus direct product and stakeholder reasoning about who a care receiver actually is.

  • Care receivers are often older adults or family members arranging care for someone elderly — a very different comfort level with apps than the carers themselves.
  • Carers repeatedly described receivers as slow to reply, unclear about what they needed, and hesitant to commit — all symptoms of one root cause: fear of messaging a stranger about a vulnerable family member.
  • That fear was rational: the old messaging design let either side start a conversation, gave no way to verify a meeting actually happened, and pushed both sides off-platform the moment things got serious — where Curam had no data and no way to help if something went wrong.
Why this section reads differently from the carer research

This isn't a claim of formal receiver interviews or usability testing — it's the design reasoning I used, built from carer-side evidence and product judgment about an anxious, often older, user base. I'm noting that distinction here deliberately, the same way I'd flag it to a hiring manager.

Who we were designing for

Carer-facing research

05

Reframing the problem

Carer-facing research

The most useful artifact from discovery wasn't a typical journey map — it was a service blueprint putting Curam's manual back-office work in the same frame as the carer's experience.

  • Documents verified by hand, overseas — forged documents could slip through.
  • Job listings with no lapse detection — carers applied to jobs already filled.
  • An unannounced 48-hour "reply or go unavailable" rule enforced by a person, not the product.
  • Client payments manually reconciled to carer payouts, one at a time.
Why this mattered strategically

Several "carer engagement" problems weren't solvable with better UI copy. Some needed the product to expose state that already existed in the backend — a distinction that shaped what we could ship in ten months versus what needed a longer conversation with engineering and operations.

From insight to roadmap

Sets up all four workstreams

Too many problems for ten months and a team of three. We prioritized against two questions: what's actually causing carers to leave or receivers to hesitate, and what can we influence without a backend rebuild?

Trust & onboarding · Carer-facing

"How might we help carers understand why each piece of information is needed — and see their own progress?"

Job discovery · Care-receiver-facing

"How might we stop carers applying to jobs that are already filled or lapsed?"

Messaging & safety · Both sides

"How might we make it safe for an anxious care receiver to message a stranger about a vulnerable family member?"

Systems & scale · Both sides

"How might we give both products one accessible design language so new features stop starting from scratch?"

08

Design & delivery

Four workstreams
A
Led design · partnered with 1 designer + engineering Carer-facing

Structuring onboarding around trust, not just data collection

  • Replaced one long form with a segmented flow: identity & right-to-work, care experience, availability, payment setup.
  • Each step explains why the information is needed and shows visible progress.
  • Designed the first version of profile-completion status shown directly to the carer, replacing a generic "please complete your profile" email.
  • Clarified DBS requirements (Basic vs. Enhanced/Standard) and what to do if circumstances change — the #1 reason carers dropped off before ever reaching a job listing.
B
Led design · partnered with 2 designers Care-receiver-facing — fixes a carer-reported problem

A care-receiver job dashboard that fixes what carers were reporting

  • Carer research found receivers posted vague job requirements and left listings open after filling them — this workstream redesigns the receiver's screens to fix both at the source.
  • Replaced "post and wait" with a guided flow: a next-step banner (Review → Message → Hire).
  • Always-visible advertised rate with plain-language fee explanation.
  • A recommended-carer list receivers can invite with one click, instead of waiting passively for applicants.
  • Added filtering by care type, language, and carer preference — the exact context carers said receivers never explained up front.
C
Led design · partnered with 1 designer Care-receiver-facing, benefits both sides

Redesigning messaging so a stranger feels safe to hire

This was the workstream with the clearest business case: every off-platform meeting or payment was a moment Curam had no data and no way to intervene if something went wrong. I designed four specific changes, each solving a named fear:

  • Only the care receiver can start a conversation — a carer can never cold-message a family. This directly answered the most common fear: an unknown carer reaching out to someone vulnerable, unprompted.
  • Voice notes for receivers who find typing a barrier — common among older users setting up care for the first time.
  • An in-app meeting scheduler (virtual or in-person) that logs the meeting to Curam — so if either side later reports an issue, the platform has a record instead of nothing.
  • Shift invoice, accept, and pay — inside the same thread. The carer sends the invoice, the receiver approves and pays through a connected third-party processor without leaving the conversation, and the thread flips to a "Hired!" state with a small celebration animation once payment clears.
Trade-off I made deliberately

Restricting carers from starting conversations reduces their sense of proactive control — a real cost, since flexibility and autonomy are the whole reason carers choose Curam. I made that trade anyway: for a platform whose growth depends on receivers trusting it enough to hire a stranger for a vulnerable family member, receiver-initiated messaging protects the relationship that matters most to retention on both sides.

D
Owned end to end Both sides

Rebuilding the design system for accessibility and speed

  • Replaced three inconsistent gray scales with one governed color ramp (Neutral, Secondary, Negative, Gray — 9–11 steps each).
  • Checked every color pairing against WCAG contrast ratios, up to AAA.
  • Defined one type scale with sizes, weights, and letter-spacing per role.
  • Specced every nav, dropdown, and menu item across 5 interaction states: default, hover, active, pressed, focused.

Design system, before & after

Both sides
Before
  • Three near-duplicate gray scales in active use, undocumented
  • Type sizes and weights set ad hoc per screen
  • No documented interaction states for navigation or menus
  • No accessibility contrast checking on any color pairing
After
  • One governed color ramp, 9–11 steps per palette
  • Single type scale: size, weight, letter-spacing per role
  • Every nav / dropdown / menu item specced across 5 states
  • Contrast ratios documented per color, up to WCAG AAA
13

Leading through the work

  • Led a team of three designers, split across the product workstreams, while personally owning the design system.
  • Ran stakeholder relationships directly with UK-based product and operations leads across a distributed, cross-timezone team.
  • Used the whiteboard sessions as the shared source of truth — so research findings, HMWs, and screen decisions stayed traceable across ten months, and a new engineer or stakeholder could walk the wall and understand the reasoning, not just the deliverable.
  • Translated research into stakeholders' own metrics — drop-off, time-to-hire, support ticket volume, dispute rate — since Curam's team was operations-first, not design-first.
  • Made and defended the receiver-initiated-messaging trade-off directly with stakeholders who worried it would frustrate proactive carers — resolved by framing it around what protects retention on the side the business depends on most: receivers willing to trust a stranger.
14

Outcomes

How impact was measured, and what changed as a direct result.

How we measured it

Google Analytics funnel reports gave a before/after view of where carers dropped off, step by step. Hotjar heatmaps and recordings on the redesigned screens confirmed whether specific pre-launch hesitation points — stalling on document upload, re-reading a field, abandoning mid-form — actually eased once the segmented flow shipped.

Onboarding

GA funnel data isolated the step carers abandoned most; Hotjar showed hesitation on document upload specifically — directly motivating the segmented, progress-visible redesign.

Job discovery

Session recordings on the old job list showed carers repeatedly opening and backing out of postings — the behavioral signature of "is this job still open?"

Messaging & trust

Every off-platform meeting or payment was a blind spot for support and dispute resolution. Logging meetings and keeping invoicing in-thread converted that blind spot into data the operations team could actually use.

  • Engineering gained a single accessible source of truth for both products, replacing three inconsistent gray scales and undocumented component states.
  • The job dashboard replaced a passive "wait for applicants" state with an explicit next-step model on both sides.
  • Messaging now gives Curam a first-party record of meetings and payments that previously happened invisibly off-platform — directly supporting dispute resolution and insurance claims.
  • The reframed service blueprint, backed by funnel and behavioral data, gave stakeholders a shared view of which problems were product issues versus operations issues — changing the roadmap conversation for two quarters.

Note: specific before/after percentages from Curam's GA and Hotjar dashboards were not retained after the engagement ended. Add the top-line drop-off number here if you're able to retrieve it — it's the single strongest number a hiring manager will look for.

15

Reflection

What I'd push harder on earlier

The service blueprint should have existed in month one, not month four — it became the most persuasive artifact in every stakeholder conversation.

What I'd advocate for next

Automated document verification and job-lapse detection are the clearest next investment for reducing both carer distrust and operational load.

A trade-off I'd revisit

Receiver-initiated-only messaging protects trust, but I'd want usage data on whether it quietly discourages proactive, in-demand carers from engaging with slow-to-reply receivers — and design a safe middle ground if so.

What held up well

Treating the whiteboard as the shared source of truth kept a distributed team and remote stakeholders aligned for ten months without re-litigating earlier decisions.
